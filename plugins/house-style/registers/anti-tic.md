# Anti-tic rules: faults readers now take for machine prose

**Scope: every register.** These are faults whoever writes them;
readers now take them for machine output, which makes them costlier.
The genre changes what counts as good prose; it does not change what
reads as machine-written. Each register names this file rather than
copying its rules, so that the copies cannot drift apart.

## The countable four

Advisory, not a veto: a flag is a prompt to look, not a defect. A
colon after a heading or a short label is fine, list or not
("Keywords: survey, settlement, and landscape"); the tell is a
sentence that announces its list ("Three things matter: X, Y, and
Z"). A count can't always tell the two apart.

| Rule | Ceiling | Why it is a tell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Announcement colons per 1,000 words | ≤ 1.6 | The colon used to announce rather than to list or define, as in an appended interpretive tag ("…: the reason X matters"). The most discriminating measure found so far |
| "Not X but Y" per 1,000 words | ≤ 3.0 | Antithesis as a recurring move; make the positive claim. Once, as a real contrast, is ordinary prose |
| Colon-led lists of three or more | 0 | A sentence that announces a list with a colon: three or more comma-separated items closing on *and* or *or*. A list after a heading or a short label doesn't count |
| Runs of three or more short sentences | 0 | Three or more sentences of five words or fewer in a row read as staccato performance. Two together may stand; a short sentence is an occasional beat, not a rhythm |

**Evidence for the colon ceiling.** In one author's measurement across
ten documents, the author's own letters read 0.00–0.91 announcement
colons per 1,000 words, and model drafts read 1.86–8.45, the worst an
email. The 1.6 ceiling, inherited from the same author's scholarly
corpus, fell on the boundary.

## The manual rules

No count sees these, which is why a clean count is not a pass.

- **Figurative language and metaphor.** Literalise.
- **Ornate parallelism**, and balanced abstract closers. A paragraph
  that ends on a tidy aphorism usually ended one sentence earlier.
- **The rule of three everywhere.** A tricolon crescendo placed for
  effect is rhetoric; the tricolon as the default shape of every list
  is the tell.
- **Definite-article grandiosity**: "*the* institution that…", "*the*
  framework for…", where "a" is true and "the" is a claim.
- **Performed candour and manufactured concessions.** Concede only
  where the counter-position has real merit, and state a real boundary
  quietly rather than announcing it ("We are candid about its reach").
- **Sentences about the document** rather than the world ("The
  diagnosis is now visible."), including self-justifying appositions
  and commentary on how the text was produced. A leading roadmap is
  fine; backward and sideways narration is not.
- **Stock additive openers** ("Moreover", "Furthermore",
  "Additionally"). A connective names a relation (cause, concession,
  sequence, or contrast), or it goes.
- **Caveats in parentheses.** Parentheses carry definitions and data
  (an acronym at first use, a count, a date), not caveats. A
  qualification that matters gets its own clause or sentence; one that
  doesn't is cut.
- **Em-dashes.** Readers now associate them with model output. Each
  register sets a ceiling. A dash that survives carries data or a
  definition, never drama, and a pair of commas or parentheses usually
  serves instead.
- **Smoothly stated unanchored specifics.** A number, name, date,
  quotation, view attributed to a named scholar, origin story, or model
  identity that can't be traced to a source is marked in the draft, in
  the form the register or skill sets. Fluent precision without a
  source is both a confabulation and a tic. Check quotations and
  attributed views as pairs: a real scholar with a plausible quotation
  that is not theirs passes a check of either alone.
- **Uniform paragraph length and sentence rhythm.** Weight the argument
  instead. Give the main point more room than the supporting ones, and
  let paragraph length follow importance; evenly weighted paragraphs
  make everything sound equally important. Human prose is lumpier, and
  should be.

## Per-register notes

- **Email:** contractions are correct register, not tics. The
  announcement colon is the live risk in email.
